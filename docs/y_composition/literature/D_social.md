# Strand D: Social impacts of bushfires/disasters, and social vulnerability versus social impact

Prepared for AUSSEF (NSW bushfires 2015–2025, fire × LGA). Every citation below was checked against Crossref and/or OpenAlex metadata (authors, year, title, venue, DOI). Abstracts were read through OpenAlex or the publisher and university pages. Verification status is shown for each entry. **[V]** means fully verified: metadata and substantive findings both checked against the abstract or an official summary. **[PV]** means partially verified; the entry says which part is unconfirmed.

## Corrections to the brief

- **Tate 2013.** The paper is "Uncertainty Analysis for a Social Vulnerability Index", *Annals of the Association of American Geographers* 103(3). It appeared online in 2012 (Crossref "issued" 2012) and in print in 2013. Cite it as Tate (2013) and keep it distinct from Tate (2012) in *Natural Hazards*.
- **Parsons et al.** The 2016 paper is the conceptual design of what it calls the *Australian Natural Disaster Resilience Index (ANDRI)*, in IJDRR 19. The 2021 paper is the national assessment under the name *Australian Disaster Resilience Index (ADRI)*, in IJDRR 62. Both exist, and ANDRI and ADRI are the same index.
- **Bryant et al. 2014 ANZJP.** Correct. The follow-up waves are Bryant et al. 2018 (ANZJP 52, Wave 2) and Bryant et al. 2021 (ANZJP 55, 10-year Wave 3; online 2020).
- **HILDA disaster-wellbeing study.** The paper you had in mind is Johnston, Önder, Rahman & Ulubaşoğlu (2021), *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* 192. Its title is "Evaluating wildfire exposure: Using wellbeing data to estimate and value the impacts of wildfire", not "The effect of natural disasters on wellbeing". A separate HILDA study covering all disasters is Sharma, Kuskoff, Parsell & Middlebrook (2026, IJDRR).
- **Deryugina, Kawano & Levitt 2018 AEJ: Applied.** Correct, vol. 10(2): 202–233, DOI 10.1257/app.20160307.
- **Smoke health figures.** Johnston et al. (2021, *Nature Sustainability* 4:42–47; online 2020) report 429 smoke-related deaths, 3,230 hospital admissions and 1,523 emergency asthma attendances, costing A$1.95bn. The earlier *MJA* letter (Borchers Arriagada et al. 2020) gave lower preliminary counts: 417 deaths, 3,151 admissions and 1,305 attendances. Cite the *Nature Sustainability* figures.

## A. Social vulnerability and resilience indices (pre-event inputs, which are candidate X variables)

### 1. Cutter, Boruff & Shirley (2003). SoVI [V]
Cutter, S.L., Boruff, B.J., & Shirley, W.L. (2003). Social vulnerability to environmental hazards. *Social Science Quarterly*, 84(2), 242–261. https://doi.org/10.1111/1540-6237.8402002
- **Summary:** Builds the Social Vulnerability Index (SoVI) for US counties from 1990 census data. Principal-components analysis reduces 42 socioeconomic and demographic variables to 11 factors (about 76% of variance), which are summed additively. The factors driving the score differ from county to county.
- **Variables:** income and poverty, age, race and ethnicity, gender, renters, housing stock, occupation, infrastructure dependence and similar. All are pre-event census measures.
- **Horizon:** cross-sectional; measures the pre-hazard state.
- **Key finding:** Vulnerability clusters in space and has many dimensions. It is a latent capacity to be harmed, not an observed harm.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** This is the template for a vulnerability X. SEIFA IRSD and remoteness play the same role as SoVI and should not enter Y.

### 2. Tate (2012). Comparative uncertainty and sensitivity analysis of SVIs [V]
Tate, E. (2012). Social vulnerability indices: a comparative assessment using uncertainty and sensitivity analysis. *Natural Hazards*, 63(2), 325–347. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11069-012-0152-2
- **Summary:** Runs global sensitivity analysis on the three common index designs: deductive, hierarchical and inductive (SoVI-type PCA). Each stage of construction is varied: indicator set, scale, transformation, normalisation and weighting.
- **Horizon:** methodological.
- **Key finding:** The hierarchical design was most accurate and the inductive design most precise. Deductive ranks are most sensitive to the transformation method. Hierarchical ranks are most sensitive to weighting, and inductive ranks to the indicator set and scale.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** The DL/IL/FP/SL Y is a hierarchical composite, so weighting is the main source of rank instability. Report Y rankings under several weighting schemes.

### 3. Tate (2013). Monte Carlo uncertainty for a hierarchical SVI [V]
Tate, E. (2013; online 2012). Uncertainty analysis for a social vulnerability index. *Annals of the Association of American Geographers*, 103(3), 526–543. https://doi.org/10.1080/00045608.2012.700616
- **Summary:** Uses Monte Carlo simulation to vary indicator selection, scale, measurement error, transformation, normalisation and weighting in a hierarchical index for census tracts. Confidence limits are computed for tract ranks.
- **Key finding:** Rank uncertainty is high. The baseline index is statistically biased in about a third of tracts, and precision falls as vulnerability rises.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** Publish confidence bands or rank intervals for Y, using a bootstrap over weights and indicator choices, especially for the most severely hit fire × LGA cells.

### 4. Cutter, Burton & Emrich (2010). BRIC [V]
Cutter, S.L., Burton, C.G., & Emrich, C.T. (2010). Disaster resilience indicators for benchmarking baseline conditions. *Journal of Homeland Security and Emergency Management*, 7(1), Article 51. https://doi.org/10.2202/1547-7355.1732
- **Summary:** Proposes the Baseline Resilience Indicators for Communities (BRIC), measuring social, economic, institutional, infrastructure and community capital for counties in the south-eastern US.
- **Key finding:** Resilience varies sharply between rural and urban areas, with metropolitan counties more resilient. What drives resilience differs by place.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** Baseline resilience is an X that moderates impact. It supports an interaction of remoteness × fire exposure, not inclusion in Y.

### 5. Parsons et al. (2016). ANDRI conceptual design [V]
Parsons, M., Glavac, S., Hastings, P., Marshall, G., McGregor, J., McNeill, J., Morley, P., Reeve, I., & Stayner, R. (2016). Top-down assessment of disaster resilience: A conceptual framework using coping and adaptive capacities. *International Journal of Disaster Risk Reduction*, 19, 1–11. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijdrr.2016.07.005
- **Summary:** Sets out seven design properties for resilience indices and designs a hierarchical, top-down index for Australia from national secondary data. Coping capacity covers social character, economic capital, infrastructure and planning, emergency services, community capital, and information and engagement. Adaptive capacity covers governance and policy, and social and community engagement.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** This is the Australian precedent for keeping capacity (an input) conceptually separate from realised loss (an outcome).

### 6. Parsons et al. (2021). ADRI national assessment [V]
Parsons, M., Reeve, I., McGregor, J., Hastings, P., Marshall, G.R., McNeill, J., Stayner, R., & Glavac, S. (2021). Disaster resilience in Australia: A geographic assessment using an index of coping and adaptive capacity. *IJDRR*, 62, 102422. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijdrr.2021.102422
- **Summary:** Applies ADRI nationally at SA2 level. About 32% of the population lives in high-capacity areas, 52% in moderate and 16% in low.
- **Key finding:** Remoteness strongly shapes resilience. Most outer regional and remote areas have low capacity, although social cohesion is often stronger there.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** The SA2-level ADRI scores can be aggregated to LGAs as an alternative or extra resilience X alongside SEIFA IRSD. They are a robustness check, not a Y component.

### 7. Rufat, Tate, Emrich & Antolini (2019). Construct validity of SV models [V]
Rufat, S., Tate, E., Emrich, C.T., & Antolini, F. (2019). How valid are social vulnerability models? *Annals of the American Association of Geographers*, 109(4), 1131–1153. https://doi.org/10.1080/24694452.2018.1535887
- **Summary:** Regresses Hurricane Sandy outcomes on four social vulnerability models, controlling for flood exposure. The outcomes are assistance applicants, affected renters, housing damage and property loss.
- **Key finding:** The indices explain housing assistance applicants best and property loss poorly. Themes on access and functional needs, age, transport and housing are most explanatory.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** This is direct evidence that vulnerability predicts social outcomes, such as assistance-seeking, better than physical loss. It supports vulnerability as X predicting SL, and treating income-support uptake as a meaningful SL outcome.

## B. Measured social outcomes after bushfires and other disasters

### 8. Gibbs et al. (2013). Beyond Bushfires study protocol [V]
Gibbs, L., Waters, E., Bryant, R.A., Pattison, P., Lusher, D., Harms, L., Richardson, J., MacDougall, C., Block, K., Snowdon, E., Gallagher, H.C., Sinnott, V., Ireton, G., & Forbes, D. (2013). Beyond Bushfires: Community, Resilience and Recovery – a longitudinal mixed method study of the medium to long term impacts of bushfires on mental health and social connectedness. *BMC Public Health*, 13, 1036. https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2458-13-1036
- **Summary:** Protocol for the cohort study of the 2009 Victorian (Black Saturday) fires. Communities are stratified as high, medium or low affected, and the study combines surveys, social-network data and interviews. The full author list is from the publisher record; Crossref lists the first eight authors.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** Establishes graded community exposure (high, medium, low) as the design. This is analogous to AUSSEF's burnt-share or severity exposure at LGA level.

### 9. Bryant et al. (2014). Beyond Bushfires Wave 1 [V]
Bryant, R.A., Waters, E., Gibbs, L., Gallagher, H.C., Pattison, P., Lusher, D., MacDougall, C., Harms, L., Block, K., Snowdon, E., Sinnott, V., Ireton, G., Richardson, J., & Forbes, D. (2014). Psychological outcomes following the Victorian Black Saturday bushfires. *Australian & New Zealand Journal of Psychiatry*, 48(7), 634–643. https://doi.org/10.1177/0004867414534476
- **Outcome variables:** fire-related and general probable PTSD (PCL), major depressive episode, heavy drinking, K6 severe distress. N = 1,017, surveyed 3–4 years after the fires.
- **Key findings:** Probable fire-related PTSD was 15.6% in high-affected communities, 7.2% in medium and 1.0% in low. Depression was 12.9%, 8.8% and 6.3%, and severe distress 9.8%, 5.0% and 4.9%. Predictors were fear for one's life, the death of someone close, and subsequent stressors, including loss of income and housing. A third of those with severe distress received no mental-health care.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** Mental-health harm follows a clear dose-response with exposure but cannot be observed in public LGA data. Acknowledge it as unmeasured SL, a likely source of underestimation.

### 10. Bryant et al. (2018). Wave 2 [V]
Bryant, R.A., Gibbs, L., Gallagher, H.C., Pattison, P., Lusher, D., MacDougall, C., Harms, L., Block, K., et al. (2018; online 2017). Longitudinal study of changing psychological outcomes following the Victorian Black Saturday bushfires. *ANZJP*, 52(6), 542–551. https://doi.org/10.1177/0004867417714337
- **Summary:** The five-year follow-up of the same cohort. I verified the metadata only and did not re-read the abstract, so no findings are quoted here. [PV for findings]
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** Shows that outcome trajectories need more than one post-period.

### 11. Bryant et al. (2021). 10-year Wave 3 [V]
Bryant, R.A., Gibbs, L., Gallagher, H.C., Pattison, P., Lusher, D., MacDougall, C., Harms, L., Block, K., et al. (2021; online 2020). The dynamic course of psychological outcomes following the Victorian Black Saturday bushfires. *ANZJP*, 55(7), 666–677. https://doi.org/10.1177/0004867420969815
- **Variables and horizon:** Waves at 3–4, 5 and 10 years (N = 1,017, 736 and 525).
- **Key findings:** Between 3–4 and 10 years, fire-related PTSD fell from 12.2% to 6.2% and severe distress from 7.5% to 4.4%. Depression (10.9% to 8.3%) and alcohol abuse (21.8% to 18.5%) were broadly stable. In high-affected areas, 22.1% had PTSD, depression or severe distress at 10 years. Worsening was linked to the extent of property loss and to later stressors. Only 24.6% of those with a probable disorder had sought help.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** Social harm is long-lived and tied to direct loss. SL windows of 1–2 years probably understate it, and the link to property loss means SL and DL will be correlated.

### 12. Gibbs et al. (2020; released March 2021). *10 Years Beyond Bushfires Report* [PV]
Gibbs, L., et al. *10 Years Beyond Bushfires Report*. University of Melbourne. https://mspgh.unimelb.edu.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0004/4165843/10-years-Beyond-Bushfires-report.pdf (see also https://www.unimelb.edu.au/newsroom/news/2021/march/bushfire-recovery-its-a-long-story-10-year-report)
- **Summary:** Synthesis report on more than 1,000 participants in 25 communities. 62.6% said they felt "mostly" or fully recovered ten years on, but many had lasting mental-health and wellbeing effects, especially where other major stressors followed.
- **Verification:** The title, lead author, institution, March 2021 release and the 62.6% figure were confirmed through the University of Melbourne newsroom. The full author list and the exact date printed in the report were not confirmed because the PDF did not download.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** Recovery is uneven and slow, which supports measuring SL at several lags (t+1 and t+2 years, and census 5-year changes).

### 13. Johnston, Önder, Rahman & Ulubaşoğlu (2021). Wildfire and wellbeing in HILDA [V]
Johnston, D.W., Önder, Y.K., Rahman, M.H., & Ulubaşoğlu, M.A. (2021). Evaluating wildfire exposure: Using wellbeing data to estimate and value the impacts of wildfire. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*, 192, 782–798. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2021.10.029 (working paper: MPRA 109652)
- **Variables and method:** Life satisfaction and its domains (including feeling safe) and mental health (SF-36/MHI-5 type measures in HILDA). Individual fixed effects on HILDA Waves 2–11 (2002–2011), with exposure defined by proximity to the 2009 Black Saturday fires.
- **Key findings:** Life satisfaction falls significantly for people living close to the fires. The loss is valued at about A$52,300, roughly 80% of a full-time Victorian worker's annual income. The largest effect is on feeling safe, and the most affected group is people with low social support. Mental-health effects appear with a delay.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** The non-market wellbeing loss is large relative to income loss, so an income-only SL is a lower bound. HILDA is not public at LGA level; cite this study to justify SL's weight rather than as a data source.

### 14. Sharma, Kuskoff, Parsell & Middlebrook (2026). HILDA disasters and wellbeing [V]
Sharma, N., Kuskoff, E., Parsell, C., & Middlebrook, N. (2026). A longitudinal study of the impact of disasters on individual wellbeing. *IJDRR*, 133, 105999. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijdrr.2026.105999 (earlier report: University of Queensland, 2024, https://doi.org/10.14264/a7a822d)
- **Variables and method:** Life satisfaction, happiness and social connectedness, with financial satisfaction as a mediator. Fixed effects on HILDA self-reported disaster damage, covering all disaster types.
- **Key findings:** Disasters lower life satisfaction and happiness beyond the year they occur, and the effect runs mainly through lower financial satisfaction. Social connectedness rises.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** Wellbeing losses operate largely through financial channels. This is a warning that income-based SL and wellbeing-based SL partly measure the same thing.

### 15. Deryugina, Kawano & Levitt (2018). Katrina and victims' tax returns [V]
Deryugina, T., Kawano, L., & Levitt, S.D. (2018). The economic impact of Hurricane Katrina on its victims: Evidence from individual tax returns. *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 10(2), 202–233. https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20160307
- **Variables:** individual wage and total income, employment and place of residence, from panel IRS returns compared with matched controls from similar unaffected cities.
- **Key findings:** Katrina had large, persistent effects on where people live, but small and transitory effects on employment and income. Within a few years victims' incomes exceeded those of the controls.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** Income effects can be short-lived and are distorted by displacement. LGA median income changes can reflect who left or arrived rather than harm to incumbents, so SL income measures carry composition bias.

### 16. Deryugina (2017). Fiscal cost of hurricanes through social insurance [V]
Deryugina, T. (2017). The fiscal cost of hurricanes: Disaster aid versus social insurance. *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*, 9(3), 168–198. https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20140296
- **Variables:** county-level per-capita government transfers, including unemployment insurance, public medical payments and other non-disaster programmes, over the 10 years after a hurricane. Estimated with event-study DiD.
- **Key finding:** Non-disaster transfers rise substantially in affected counties over the following decade, and their present value is well above direct disaster aid.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** This is the closest methodological precedent for "excess income-support recipients per 1,000 versus unburnt councils". It also shows that the same transfers are a **fiscal** cost, so the DSS indicator sits on the SL/FP boundary (see the double-counting section below).

### 17. Borgschulte, Molitor & Zou (2024). Wildfire smoke and earnings [V]
Borgschulte, M., Molitor, D., & Zou, E.Y. (2024). Air pollution and the labor market: Evidence from wildfire smoke. *Review of Economics and Statistics*, 106(6), 1558–1575. https://doi.org/10.1162/rest_a_01243
- **Variables:** county-quarter earnings and employment (Quarterly Workforce Indicators), linked to satellite smoke-plume days.
- **Key findings:** Each extra smoke day reduces quarterly earnings per capita by about 0.1%. Extensive-margin responses (lower employment, labour-force exit) account for about 13% of the loss. Smoke effects reach well beyond burnt areas.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** Smoke spillovers mean "unburnt" comparison councils may also be treated, which biases excess-SL estimates towards zero. Consider excluding smoke-affected neighbours from the controls, or adding a smoke-exposure covariate.

### 18. Johnston et al. (2021). Smoke PM2.5 health costs of Black Summer [V]
Johnston, F.H., Borchers-Arriagada, N., Morgan, G.G., Jalaludin, B., Palmer, A.J., Williamson, G.J., & Bowman, D.M.J.S. (2021; online 2020). Unprecedented health costs of smoke-related PM2.5 from the 2019–20 Australian megafires. *Nature Sustainability*, 4, 42–47. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41893-020-00610-5
- **Variables and method:** premature deaths, cardiovascular and respiratory hospital admissions, and emergency asthma attendances attributed to fire-smoke PM2.5 through concentration-response functions, then monetised. Covers the fire seasons from 2000–01 to 2019–20.
- **Key findings:** The 2019–20 season caused about 429 deaths, 3,230 admissions and 1,523 emergency asthma attendances, costing about A$1.95bn. That is far above any earlier season (next highest A$566m in 2002–03) and more than nine times the 19-year median of A$211m.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** Smoke health cost is a major social loss that is spatially decoupled from burnt area, mostly falling on Sydney and other cities. If it enters SL it must be allocated by smoke exposure, not fire footprint, and that fits poorly in a fire × LGA design.

### 19. Hickson & Marshan (2022). Bushfires, floods and gendered labour-market effects in Australia [V]
Hickson, J., & Marshan, J. (2022). Labour market effects of bushfires and floods in Australia: A gendered perspective. *Economic Record*, 98(S1), 1–23. https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-4932.12688
- **Variables:** individual employment and labour supply (HILDA), linked to regional bushfire and flood events over about two decades.
- **Key findings:** Bushfires lower the probability of female employment by 1.6 percentage points (about 5,000 jobs a year) while raising male employment in mining and transport. There is evidence of an "added worker" effect within households.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** Aggregate LGA income or recipient counts can hide offsetting gender effects. Note this as a limitation, or use ABS Census employment by sex as a secondary SL check.

## C. Distributional and equity evidence in Australia

### 20. Akter & Grafton (2021). Do fires discriminate? [V]
Akter, S., & Grafton, R.Q. (2021). Do fires discriminate? Socio-economic disadvantage, wildfire hazard exposure and the Australian 2019–20 'Black Summer' fires. *Climatic Change*, 165(3–4), 53. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10584-021-03064-6
- **Method:** links NIAFE fire extent to SEIFA 2016. Exposure is the share of area burnt interacted with proximity to settlements.
- **Key finding:** Exposure rises significantly with disadvantage in inner regional, outer regional and remote NSW and Victoria. The authors suggest resource gaps in suppression and hazard reduction as a possible reason.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** IRSD is correlated with exposure itself. If IRSD is an X, check for collinearity with fire severity and treat IRSD as a confounder of the exposure–SL relationship. This is a strong reason to keep it out of Y.

### 21. Akter & Grafton (2025). Socioeconomic wellbeing losses of Black Summer [V]
Akter, S., & Grafton, R.Q. (2025). Socioeconomic well-being losses of Australia's Black Summer fires (2019–2020): Burden by burned area, poverty, and gender. *One Earth*, 8(11), 101454. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.oneear.2025.101454
- **Variables and method:** ABS Census 2016 to 2021 changes for small areas (about 400 people, roughly SA1), burnt versus matched unburnt, estimated by DiD. Outcomes include income (share of households with nil or negative income), rent and housing, and unpaid work (at least 15 hours a week) by sex. "Poor" means the bottom half of IRSD. Design details were confirmed from the authors' summary in *The Conversation*.
- **Key findings:** Well-being declined in the economic, housing and unpaid-work domains in the most-burnt areas. In severely burnt peri-urban poor areas, the share of households with nil or negative income rose by about 1.3 percentage points. Rents rose by about A$20 a week (about 10%) across burnt areas and A$26 a week (about 13%) in poor areas. Among women in highly burnt areas, the share doing heavy unpaid work rose by about 1.7 percentage points, against about 1 point for men.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** This is the closest existing analogue to AUSSEF's SL: public ABS data, burnt versus unburnt, excess change. It supports adding **housing stress or median rent change** (Census) as a third SL indicator, and using IRSD as an effect modifier (X × exposure), not a Y component.

### 22. Akter (2023). Black Summer recovery measured with nightlights in NSW [V]
Akter, S. (2023). Australia's Black Summer wildfires recovery: A difference-in-differences analysis using nightlights. *Global Environmental Change*, 83, 102743. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gloenvcha.2023.102743
- **Variables:** monthly night-time radiance by mesh block across NSW, January 2017 to June 2021, cross-checked against Facebook movement data.
- **Key findings:** Affected areas saw about a 30% fall in economic activity in major cities and inner-regional areas. Recovery was slower in rural hinterlands, and poor city communities lagged non-poor ones. Nightlights do not work in forested remote areas.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** This is a NSW-specific precedent for a burnt versus unburnt DiD. It belongs to IL (activity) rather than SL, but confirms that disadvantage slows recovery.

### 23. Williamson, Markham & Weir (2020). Aboriginal peoples and the 2019–20 bushfires [V]
Williamson, B., Markham, F., & Weir, J.K. (2020). *Aboriginal peoples and the response to the 2019–2020 bushfires*. CAEPR Working Paper No. 134/2020, Centre for Aboriginal Economic Policy Research, ANU. https://cipr.cass.anu.edu.au/research/publications/aboriginal-peoples-and-response-2019-2020-bushfires (PDF also on AIDR: https://www.aidr.org.au/media/7718/aboriginal-people-and-the-response-to-the-2019-20-bushfires.pdf)
- **Summary:** Maps the Aboriginal population and land rights in fire-affected NSW and Victoria.
- **Key findings:** More than 84,000 Indigenous people, about a quarter of the Indigenous population of the two states, lived in the fire-affected area, making up about 5.4% of the 1.55 million affected residents. Their share among the affected is disproportionate, and recovery responses paid little attention to them.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** Indigenous population share (Census) is a defensible vulnerability X or heterogeneity split. Cultural and Country losses are an SL component that public LGA data cannot capture.

## D. Combining social and economic indicators

### 24. OECD/JRC (2008). *Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators* [V]
Nardo, M., Saisana, M., Saltelli, A., Tarantola, S., Hoffmann, A., & Giovannini, E. (2008). *Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators: Methodology and User Guide*. OECD Publishing / European Commission JRC. https://doi.org/10.1787/9789264043466-en
- **Summary:** The standard guide to composite indicators. Its 10 steps run from the theoretical framework and variable selection, through imputation, multivariate analysis, normalisation, weighting and aggregation, to uncertainty and sensitivity analysis and links to other variables. It warns that correlated sub-indicators given equal weights implicitly double-weight the shared dimension, and that additive aggregation implies full compensability between dimensions.
- **Verification note:** Crossref returns the 2008 book DOI without author metadata; authorship is taken from the 2005 OECD working-paper version (DOI 10.1787/533411815016) and the standard citation.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** Check the correlations between DL, IL, FP and SL before weighting. Where SL and IL share an income signal, either drop one version or use weights that correct for correlation (for example PCA or reduced weight), and report the sensitivity.

### 25. Hallegatte, Vogt-Schilb, Bangalore & Rozenberg (2017). *Unbreakable* [V]
Hallegatte, S., Vogt-Schilb, A., Bangalore, M., & Rozenberg, J. (2017). *Unbreakable: Building the Resilience of the Poor in the Face of Natural Disasters*. World Bank, Climate Change and Development Series. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1003-9 (Crossref lists 2016; the print edition is 2017)
- **Summary:** Separates *asset losses* (what DL measures) from *well-being losses*, which weight losses by the capacity to cope, so that a dollar lost by a poor household counts for more. It argues that asset-loss totals understate the burden on poor people.
- **Implication for AUSSEF SL:** This gives SL a principled role distinct from DL. SL should capture how losses translate into hardship, not re-count dollar losses. Equity weighting belongs in how SL is defined or interpreted, not in adding IRSD to Y.

## Synthesis for AUSSEF

### 1. SL indicators that can be defended with public LGA-level data

| Indicator | Source and frequency | Literature support | Caveats |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Excess change in working-age income-support recipients per 1,000** (JobSeeker/Newstart, Youth Allowance (other), and possibly Disability Support Pension) versus unburnt councils | DSS payment demographics, quarterly, LGA | Deryugina 2017 (transfers rise for a decade); Rufat et al. 2019 (vulnerability predicts assistance-seeking); Akter & Grafton 2025 (rise in nil or negative income) | COVID-19 (JobSeeker Coronavirus Supplement from 2020Q2) confounds the Black Summer post-period badly. Use quarter × state fixed effects and end the pre/post window at 2020Q1, or difference against the national trend. Disaster Recovery Allowance and AGDRP are one-off payments that may not appear in the recipient counts. |
| **Excess change in median personal or household income** | ABS Personal Income in Australia (ATO-based, annual, LGA) or Census (5-yearly) | Deryugina et al. 2018; Akter & Grafton 2025 | Transitory (Katrina); affected by migration and composition; overlaps IL (see section 3). |
| **Excess change in median weekly rent or housing stress** (candidate addition) | Census 2016 to 2021, and NSW Rent and Sales Report (quarterly, LGA bonds) | Akter & Grafton 2025 (+A$20–26 a week) | Captures displacement and housing scarcity, which Beyond Bushfires links to later distress. The quarterly NSW bond data avoids the 5-year Census gap. |
| Population change or outward migration (optional) | ABS Estimated Resident Population, annual, LGA | Deryugina et al. 2018 (large, persistent relocation) | Ambiguous sign: departures may be adaptive. Better used as a diagnostic of composition bias than as a Y component. |
| Smoke-attributable health burden | Johnston et al. 2021 | Large A$ losses | **Not suitable for a fire × LGA Y.** Smoke exposure does not follow the burnt footprint and would dominate urban LGAs. Keep it as a separately reported, state-level cost. |
| Mental health (PTSD, depression, distress) and life satisfaction | Beyond Bushfires; HILDA | Large and persistent (Bryant 2014, 2021; Johnston 2021) | No public LGA series exists. State this as a known omission, meaning Y is a lower bound on social loss. (Medicare mental-health item counts by region are a possible future proxy; I did not verify that they are available at LGA level.) |

### 2. Vulnerability as X versus impact as Y

- The literature consistently treats SoVI, BRIC, ADRI and SEIFA as **pre-event capacity or susceptibility** measures (Cutter 2003, 2010; Parsons 2016, 2021). Validation work regresses outcomes *on* them (Rufat et al. 2019). This supports AUSSEF's plan to put IRSD and remoteness in X and keep them out of Y.
- Disadvantage is correlated with exposure itself (Akter & Grafton 2021) and modifies impact (Akter & Grafton 2025; Akter 2023; Johnston et al. 2021 on low social support). Model IRSD both as a confounder and as an interaction with severity. Putting it in Y would build the predictor into the outcome, so the X–Y relationship would hold by construction.
- Measure SL as a **change relative to controls** (excess versus unburnt), never as a level. Levels of income or welfare receipt mostly reflect pre-existing vulnerability, which is exactly the thing that should stay in X.

### 3. Double-counting cautions

1. **Income in IL and SL.** If IL includes lost earnings, wages or business income, "change in median personal income" in SL is largely the same signal. Options: (a) define IL from business and output measures (turnover, business counts, GRP, nightlights) and keep household income only in SL; (b) keep income in IL only and use distributional measures in SL (income-support recipients, rent or housing stress); or (c) keep both but check the correlation and down-weight them (OECD/JRC 2008). Option (b) fits Hallegatte et al. best, since SL then measures hardship rather than dollars.
2. **Income support in SL and FP.** Extra JobSeeker receipt is both a social outcome (more people in hardship) and a Commonwealth fiscal cost (Deryugina 2017). If FP is defined as *local or state government* fiscal pressure (council revenue and expenses, DRFA claims), the overlap is small. If FP includes Commonwealth transfers, count them in only one place. Record the choice explicitly.
3. **SL and DL correlation.** Mental-health and wellbeing harm scales with property loss (Bryant 2021). Correlation between SL and DL is expected and is not double-counting in itself. It does mean equal-weight additive aggregation will over-reward LGAs with large DL. Run the weighting sensitivity recommended by Tate (2012, 2013).
4. **Composition and displacement.** Income and recipient rates per 1,000 residents change when population changes (Deryugina et al. 2018). Report ERP change alongside SL, and consider fixed denominators set before the fire.
5. **Contaminated controls.** Smoke (Borgschulte et al. 2024) and COVID-19 affect "unburnt" councils too. Choose controls away from the fire front and use state × quarter time effects.
6. **Uncertainty.** Publish Y rank intervals from a Monte Carlo over weights, normalisation and indicator inclusion (Tate 2013; OECD/JRC 2008).
