## A. International empirical literature

### 1. Jerch, Kahn & Lin (2023)
**Citation:** Jerch, R., Kahn, M. E., & Lin, G. C. (2023). Local public finance dynamics and hurricane shocks. *Journal of Urban Economics*, 134, 103516. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jue.2022.103516 (WP: NBER w28050, https://doi.org/10.3386/w28050). **Verified** (Crossref and NBER full text).

**Summary:** Panel fixed-effects study of more than 2,000 US Atlantic and Gulf coast municipalities since 1980. Treatment is maximum hurricane wind speed. Fiscal data come from the Census of Governments, and default risk from Moody's Analytics bond ratings. Effects are split into a short run (years 1–5) and a long run (years 6–10).

**Fiscal variables:** total, own-source and tax revenue per capita; intergovernmental transfers; expenditure by function (public works, etc.); total debt; bond ratings and default risk.

**Timing:** Intergovernmental transfers rise **in the short run (≤5 yrs, +2.3% per 1 SD wind speed)** and mask the damage at first. Own-source revenue losses are **concentrated in years 6–10 (about −2% per SD; −7.2% over the decade for major hurricanes)**. Public-works spending falls 3.4% in years 6–10. **Debt falls immediately** (−19% to −26% over 10 years after a major hurricane) as ratings are downgraded.

**Key findings:** Local fiscal damage is slow-burning and persistent. Transfers hide it at first. Spending moves nearly in step with revenue, so the adjustment happens through services. Effects are more than twice as large in minority-heavy places.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** Look beyond the event FY. Test t+1…t+3 at least, and expect transfers to mask own-source weakness early on.

### 2. Liao & Kousky (2022)
**Citation:** Liao, Y., & Kousky, C. (2022). The fiscal impacts of wildfires on California municipalities. *Journal of the Association of Environmental and Resource Economists*, 9(3), 455–493. https://doi.org/10.1086/717492. **Verified** (Crossref/OpenAlex). Numbers below are from the author's working-paper draft (yanjunliao.com), so check them against the published tables.

**Summary:** Event study and difference-in-differences on California municipalities, 1990–2015. Treated municipalities are those with ≥10% of area burned. Controls are municipalities that burn later ("not-yet-treated"). Data are CAL FIRE perimeters plus State Controller finance reports. Outcomes are followed up to 5 years after the fire.

**Fiscal variables:** general revenue (property, sales and transfer taxes); functional revenue (special taxes, service charges, intergovernmental transfers); expenditure by function (public safety, community development, transportation); **excess revenue per capita** (revenue − expenditure); **probability of a budget deficit**.

**Timing:** The authors lag fire by one year because of the FY mismatch: the CA fiscal year runs Jul–Jun and fire season falls after it closes. Functional revenue **falls about 7% in the fire year**, then rises 13–18% in years 1–4 via voter-approved special taxes, which arrive with a one-year lag. Spending on community development and public safety rises **persistently**, not as a one-off spike.

**Key findings:** Revenue rises 10.5% but expenditure rises 17.3% (community development +40%, public safety +18.5%, transport +17.8%). The net effect is **−$97 per capita excess revenue (≈10.7% of budget) and +25 pp probability of a deficit**. Intergovernmental transfers to municipalities are **unchanged**. Municipalities are still "surprisingly insulated" compared with state and federal suppression costs.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** This is the closest analogue: wildfire, municipal level, not-yet-treated controls. It supports an operating-balance deficit indicator and function-level spending reallocation, and it warns about fire-year versus FY alignment.

### 3. Deryugina (2017)
**Citation:** Deryugina, T. (2017). The fiscal cost of hurricanes: Disaster aid versus social insurance. *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*, 9(3), 168–198. https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20140296. **Verified.**

**Summary:** County-level event study of US hurricanes, 1979–2002, following transfers for 10 years. It separates explicit disaster aid from "hidden" increases in general social-insurance transfers: unemployment insurance, public medical payments, income maintenance.

**Fiscal variables:** per-capita federal transfers by program (county BEA transfer data), direct disaster aid, and employment.

**Timing:** Non-disaster transfers stay elevated **for about a decade**. Disaster aid is concentrated in the first years.

**Key findings:** Direct disaster aid is about $155–160 per capita. Non-disaster transfers add **$780–1,150 per capita in present value**. Hurricane transfer benefits of $44–65 billion sit on top of $19 billion in explicit federal hurricane spending. The fiscal cost of disasters is badly underestimated if only disaster programs are counted.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** Much of the fiscal cost falls on **higher tiers** (Commonwealth income support, Medicare, DRFA). Council ratios capture only part of FP. This is an argument for an FP component or note that goes beyond OLG ratios (e.g., DRFA/AGDRP payments per capita if available).

### 4. Miao, Hou & Abrigo (2018)
**Citation:** Miao, Q., Hou, Y., & Abrigo, M. (2018). Measuring the financial shocks of natural disasters: A panel study of U.S. states. *National Tax Journal*, 71(1), 11–44. https://doi.org/10.17310/ntj.2018.1.01. **Verified.** Crossref lists the main title; the subtitle is as commonly cited.

**Summary:** Panel VAR on 50 states, 1970–2013, relating disaster damage (SHELDUS) to state revenue, expenditure, debt issuance and federal transfers, with impulse responses over several years.

**Fiscal variables:** program expenditure; tax revenue by source (sales, income, property); debt issuance; federal–state transfers.

**Timing:** The response is dynamic, with spending and transfers rising in the years after the shock. Tax revenue shows amplified volatility rather than a level shift.

**Key findings:** States raise program spending, and this is **largely financed by federal transfers**, including non-disaster welfare assistance. The effect on total tax revenue is limited.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** At a sub-national tier, extra spending is mostly transfer-funded. Expect a rise in grant dependence (own-source revenue share falls) rather than a collapse in own revenue.

### 5. Miao, Abrigo, Hou & Liao (2023)
**Citation:** Miao, Q., Abrigo, M., Hou, Y., & Liao, Y. (2023). Extreme weather events and local fiscal responses: Evidence from U.S. counties. *Economics of Disasters and Climate Change*, 7(1), 93–115. https://doi.org/10.1007/s41885-022-00120-y (online Dec 2022). **Verified.**

**Summary:** Heterogeneity-robust event study of US county finances after flood and hurricane presidential disaster declarations (PDDs).

**Fiscal variables:** tax revenue, expenditure, intergovernmental revenue, borrowing.

**Timing:** Event-time dynamics with long-run effects, reported as a multi-year event window.

**Key findings:** A PDD **lowers tax revenue and raises spending and intergovernmental revenue**. Hurricanes are far worse than floods for revenue and borrowing. **Low-income and high-social-vulnerability counties lose tax revenue and borrow more.** Richer counties gain revenue and attract *more* transfers.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** The disaster *declaration* is a valid treatment definition (cf. NSW DRFA-declared LGAs). Expect the same fire to produce heterogeneous FP by council capacity, so interact with socio-economic status or council size.

### 6. Noy & Nualsri (2011)
**Citation:** Noy, I., & Nualsri, A. (2011). Fiscal storms: Public spending and revenues in the aftermath of natural disasters. *Environment and Development Economics*, 16(1), 113–128. https://doi.org/10.1017/S1355770X1000046X. **Verified.**

**Summary:** Panel VAR on quarterly fiscal data for a large cross-country panel, controlling for the business cycle.

**Fiscal variables:** government spending, revenue, and the fiscal balance/debt.

**Timing:** Quarterly impulse responses over the following quarters and years.

**Key findings:** Developed countries respond **counter-cyclically** (spending up, revenue down, so a larger deficit). Developing countries respond pro-cyclically.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** In a high-capacity federation the expected sign is **higher spending and a weaker balance**, not austerity. For well-resourced councils, crowd-out may show up as reallocation rather than cuts.

### 7. Melecky & Raddatz (2015), and Raddatz & Melecky (2011) WP
**Citation:** Melecky, M., & Raddatz, C. (2015). Fiscal responses after catastrophes and the enabling role of financial development. *World Bank Economic Review*, 29(1), 129–149. https://doi.org/10.1093/wber/lht041. Earlier WP: Raddatz, C., & Melecky, M. (2011). *How do governments respond after catastrophes? Natural-disaster shocks and the fiscal stance.* World Bank Policy Research WP 5564. https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-5564. **Both verified.**

**Summary:** Panel VAR, annual data, high- and middle-income countries, 1975–2008, by disaster type.

**Fiscal variables:** expenditure, revenue, deficit; interactions with debt-market depth and insurance penetration.

**Timing:** Annual impulse responses; deficit effects build over several years.

**Key findings:** On average, deficits rise only after *climatic* disasters, which includes fire and drought. Deeper debt markets allow larger deficit-financed responses. High **insurance penetration** reduces real losses *without* deficit expansion.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** Insurance and pre-arranged funding (DRFA acts as quasi-insurance for councils) substitute for local borrowing. Do not expect debt-service deterioration where DRFA pays.

### 8. Chen (2020)
**Citation:** Chen, G. (2020). Assessing the financial impact of natural disasters on local governments. *Public Budgeting & Finance*, 40(1), 22–44. https://doi.org/10.1111/pbaf.12245. **Verified.**

**Summary:** Panel of New York State city and county governments, 1996–2012, testing disaster effects on financial-condition ratios and whether fiscal institutions moderate them.

**Fiscal variables:** **liquidity, fund balance (reserves), debt** ratios. These are conceptually the same family as the OLG cash-expense-cover and debt-service ratios.

**Timing:** Annual panel.

**Key findings:** Once unreserved fund balance and disaster reserves are controlled for, **financial-condition ratios are not significantly affected by disasters**. Reserves are what matter.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** Expect **weak or null average effects on stock ratios** such as cash cover and debt service. Pre-event reserves (cash cover at t−1) should be a moderator or control, not just an outcome.

### 9. Bayar & Yarbrough (2024)
**Citation:** Bayar, O., & Yarbrough, T. R. (2024). The fiscal consequences of natural disasters: Evidence from the U.S. states. *Public Finance Review*, 52(2), 222–252. https://doi.org/10.1177/10911421231179535 (online 2023). **Verified.**

**Summary:** US states, 1970–2015; spending, revenue and federal transfers.

**Key findings:** A broad dollar-damage measure has **small** effects. A **major-disaster-occurrence** measure raises spending and transfers, and spending effects **grow with severity**.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** The results depend on how the treatment is measured. Use severity gradients (area burnt share, houses lost) and not only a binary burnt/unburnt indicator.

### 10. Hou & Chen (2026), meta-analysis
**Citation:** Hou, X., & Chen, C. (2026). The fiscal shocks of natural disasters: A meta-analysis. *Public Finance Review* (online first). https://doi.org/10.1177/10911421261446690. **Verified** (Crossref/OpenAlex, 2026; volume and pages not yet assigned).

**Summary:** 1,438 effect sizes from 43 studies.

**Key findings:** The overall effect on public finances is significantly negative, **driven by higher expenditure and higher intergovernmental transfers**, with *lower* debt. Average effects on **own-source revenue and budget balance are not significant**. Heterogeneity is large by outcome type, disaster measure and specification.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** This is the strongest evidence base for **expenditure-side and transfer-dependence indicators**. Own-revenue and balance measures are noisier.

### 11. Auh, Choi, Deryugina & Park (2022), working paper
**Citation:** Auh, J. K., Choi, J., Deryugina, T., & Park, T. (2022). *Natural disasters and municipal bonds.* NBER Working Paper 30280. https://doi.org/10.3386/w30280. **Verified as a WP.** I found no journal version.

**Summary:** Repeat-sales returns on US municipal bonds after disasters.

**Key findings:** Returns on uninsured bonds fall gradually over the weeks after a disaster (−0.31% on average, about $10 billion in investor losses). Effects are moderated by revenue source, bond insurance, severity, **federal disaster aid**, and local financial condition.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** This is a market-based stress channel, but not applicable: NSW councils borrow mainly through TCorp or banks, with no traded muni market. Use it only as background showing that transfers dampen perceived default risk.

## B. Australian institutional and policy sources

### 12. Productivity Commission (2014), *Natural Disaster Funding Arrangements*
**Citation:** Productivity Commission. (2014). *Natural Disaster Funding Arrangements* (Inquiry Report No. 74, Vols 1–2). Canberra. Sent to Government 17 Dec 2014, released 1 May 2015. https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries/completed/disaster-funding/report. **Verified** (full text of Vol. 1 read).

**Summary:** Review of Commonwealth–state disaster cost sharing under NDRRA. It finds the arrangements "not efficient, equitable or sustainable": they are prone to cost-shifting, over-weight recovery relative to mitigation, and make the Commonwealth the "insurer of first resort" for state and local roads.

**Fiscal facts:** 2002-03 to 2012-13: Commonwealth post-disaster spending was over $8 billion (NDRRA about $6.5 billion, AGDRP $1.3 billion). A further **≥$5.6 billion of NDRRA-eligible spending was borne by states and, implicitly, local government** net of reimbursement. Thresholds: small disaster criterion $240k; first threshold **0.225% of state revenue and grants**; second threshold 1.75× the first. Reimbursement is 50% or 75%. Most local roads are **uninsured**, so councils receive "zero-cost" disaster insurance through NDRRA. **Day-labour restrictions** and rare use of betterment distort council recovery. Many councils' revenue-raising is limited by ratepayers' **capacity to pay**, especially in large sparse remote LGAs.

**Timing:** Reimbursement follows state and council spending (claims are acquitted after the event), creating cash-flow lags.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** Reconstruction of council essential public assets is largely reimbursed. FP should therefore focus on the **unreimbursed and timing components**: cash drawdown, operating-expense spikes such as clean-up, and reallocation. Treat DRFA receipts as a confounder or mask.

### 13. Disaster Recovery Funding Arrangements 2018 (DRFA)
**Citation:** Commonwealth of Australia. (2018). *Disaster Recovery Funding Arrangements 2018* (effective 1 Nov 2018, replacing NDRRA). https://www.disasterassist.gov.au/disaster-arrangements/disaster-recovery-funding-arrangements. **Partially verified.** The agency page exists, but the determination PDF returned 403. Up-to-75% Commonwealth cost share and Categories A–D are confirmed via NEMA/QRA search snippets. The threshold mechanics above are verified for NDRRA via the PC report and were carried over into DRFA, but that carry-over is not independently confirmed from the DRFA text.

**Summary:** Categories: A, emergency assistance to individuals; B, restoration of essential public assets including council roads and bridges, plus counter-disaster operations; C, community recovery packages; D, exceptional circumstances. Funds flow Commonwealth → state (NSW Reconstruction Authority / TfNSW) → councils.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** Council reconstruction shows up as **grants and contributions revenue**, often capital. This mechanically **lowers the OLG own-source revenue ratio**, and is excluded from the operating performance ratio numerator. Interpret signs with care (see synthesis).

### 14. Royal Commission into National Natural Disaster Arrangements (2020)
**Citation:** Binskin, M., Bennett, A., & Macintosh, A. (2020). *Royal Commission into National Natural Disaster Arrangements: Report.* Commonwealth of Australia, 28 Oct 2020. https://www.royalcommission.gov.au/natural-disasters. **Verified** (full PDF read).

**Summary:** Inquiry into the 2019–20 Black Summer (80 recommendations). Ch. 11 finds that council capability and capacity depend on "relative size, natural disaster risk profile, demographics and the resources available". Councils with **large footprints, high risk and few resources struggled in 2019–20**. Small rural shires such as Indigo and Towong describe an unfair load of relief and recovery. Ch. 22 finds the DRFA "does not incentivise or prioritise resilience".

**Recommendations:** **Rec 11.1**: states should take responsibility for the capability and capacity of the local governments to which they delegate disaster responsibilities. **22.3**: review the small disaster criterion and thresholds. **22.6**: build back better within the DRFA. **22.8**: simpler DRFA application processes.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** This supports treating **council capacity (size, rate base, reserves) as a moderator** of fiscal stress. The same fire strains small rural councils more.

### 15. ANAO (2023), *Administration of the Disaster Recovery Funding Arrangements*
**Citation:** Australian National Audit Office. (2023). *Administration of the Disaster Recovery Funding Arrangements* (Auditor-General Report No. 23 of 2022–23). https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/administration-the-disaster-recovery-funding-arrangements. **Verified** (report number and headline figures via ANAO page and search; full text not read).

### 16. Audit Office of NSW (2021), *Report on Local Government 2020*
**Citation:** Audit Office of New South Wales. (2021). *Report on Local Government 2020* (financial audit of FY2019-20). https://www.audit.nsw.gov.au/our-work/reports/report-on-local-government-2020. **Verified** (agency page).

**Key facts:** 96 councils were affected by bushfires and storms in 2019-20, and 57 were in bushfire declarations. ### 17. Audit Office of NSW (2023), *Natural disasters*
**Citation:** Audit Office of New South Wales. (2023, 1 June). *Natural disasters.* https://www.audit.nsw.gov.au/our-work/reports/natural-disasters. **Verified** (agency page).

**Key facts (FY2021-22, mainly floods):** 83 of 128 LGAs were affected. Estimated damage to council infrastructure was **$349 million**. Councils spent **$128 million** on clean-up, emergency response and other costs. Councils received $162 million in grants, and **the financial impact in 2021-22 exceeded grant funding received in that year**. Effects on council accounts: higher operating expenses, asset impairment and write-offs, and lost user-charge revenue.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** This is direct evidence that **in the event FY costs exceed reimbursement**, which appears as an operating-result and cash hit, with catch-up grants in later FYs.

### 18. NSW Legislative Council Standing Committee on State Development (2024), Report 52
**Citation:** Standing Committee on State Development, Legislative Council, Parliament of NSW. (2024, November). *Ability of local governments to fund infrastructure and services* (Report 52). https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/parliamentary-business/committees/inquiry-details?committeeInquiryId=3040. **Verified** (full PDF read).

**Key facts:** OLG evidence says councils must "divert critical funds and resources" to disaster recovery. Although costs are partly reclaimable, "there is a cashflow impact" and **other works are delayed or postponed**. Councils must fund clean-up and repairs up front. Tenterfield had to "totally deplete its internal reserves" while awaiting claims more than a year after the event. There is **no disaster provision in the rate peg** (REROC). IPWEA reports grant and disaster funding inflating capital programs to 2–3× the normal annual capital spend. IPART and OLG flag accelerated **depreciation** from disaster damage.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** This is the strongest Australian support for three indicators: **cash expense cover (liquidity drawdown)**, **crowd-out/deferral of other works**, and **t+1/t+2 reimbursement lags**.

### 19. Audit Office of NSW (2022), *Local government business and service continuity arrangements for natural disasters*
**Citation:** Audit Office of New South Wales. (2022, 17 February). *Local government business and service continuity arrangements for natural disasters* (performance audit; Bega Valley and Snowy Valleys councils). https://www.audit.nsw.gov.au/our-work/reports/local-government-business-and-service-continuity-arrangements-for-natural-disasters. **Verified** (agency page).

### 20. Drew & Dollery (2016), two papers on NSW council ratios
**Citations:**
(a) Drew, J., & Dollery, B. (2016). A factor analytic assessment of financial sustainability: The case of New South Wales local government. *Australian Accounting Review*, 26(2), 132–140. https://doi.org/10.1111/auar.12092. **Verified.**
(b) Drew, J., & Dollery, B. (2016). How high should they jump? An empirical method for setting municipal financial ratio performance benchmarks. *Australian Journal of Public Administration*, 75(1), 53–64. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8500.12152. **Verified.** See also Drew & Dollery (2016), "Less haste, more speed: The Fit for the Future reform program in NSW local government", *AJPA* 75(1): 78–88, https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8500.12158.

**Implication for AUSSEF FP:** Do **not** simply sum OLG ratios into one FP score. Standardise within council peer groups (OLG categories), consider factor or PCA weights, and measure **changes vs own baseline and vs peer councils** rather than benchmark breaches.

## C. Reference: OLG ratio definitions (as used by AUSSEF)
Source: Your Council NSW / OLG, https://yourcouncil.nsw.gov.au/nsw-overview/finances/ (verified).

| OLG ratio | Definition (abridged) | Benchmark | Effect of DRFA and disaster accounting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating performance ratio | (operating revenue **excl. capital grants and contributions** − operating expenses) / operating revenue excl. capital grants | ≥ 0% | Clean-up and emergency costs raise expenses. Operating (not capital) DRFA grants for emergency works offset them, possibly in a later FY. Impairment and depreciation from damaged assets lower it. |
| Own-source operating revenue ratio | (operating revenue − **all** grants and contributions) / operating revenue **incl.** capital grants | ≥ 60% | **Mechanically falls when DRFA and recovery grants arrive**, so it measures transfer dependence rather than own-revenue loss. |
| Debt service cover ratio (the brief's "debt service ratio") | operating result before interest and depreciation, excl. capital grants / (principal + interest) | > 2× | Higher is *better*. Needs sign alignment in Y. Most councils have little debt, so there are many zeros or extreme values. |
| Cash expense cover ratio | (cash + equivalents + term deposits) / monthly operating and financing payments | > 3 months | Captures the **upfront-funding and reserve-drawdown** channel (Report 52, Audit Office 2023). |

## Synthesis for AUSSEF FP

### 1. Which FP indicators are best supported
1. **Expenditure-side pressure and reallocation (budget crowd-out):** strongest support. Meta-analysis: expenditure is the main driver (Hou & Chen 2026). Liao & Kousky find persistent rises in community-development and public-safety spending after wildfires. Jerch et al. find cuts to public works. NSW Report 52 records works "delayed or postponed". AUSSEF's community-services vs roads-and-bridges shares fit this, but one refinement matters. In NSW, recovery spending mainly goes to **roads and bridges** (DRFA Cat B), so the *roads share rising and other functions' shares falling* is the crowd-out signature. Define the sign accordingly and consider an "other services share" measure.
2. **Liquidity / cash expense cover:** well supported for Australia specifically. Councils pre-fund repairs and wait a year or more for reimbursement (Report 52; Audit Office 2023: impact > grants in the event year). US evidence (Chen 2020) shows reserves determine resilience. Use Δcash cover (t vs t−1) and treat pre-event cash cover as a moderator.
3. **Operating balance (operating performance ratio):** supported. Liao & Kousky find −$97 per capita excess revenue and +25 pp deficit probability. Audit Office 2023 documents operating-expense spikes and impairments. It is noisy because of reimbursement timing and depreciation.
4. **Own-source revenue share:** keep it, but **reinterpret** it as *grant/transfer dependence*. A fall reflects DRFA inflows as much as lost rates or fees. Literature consistently finds transfers up and own-source revenue down little or not significantly in the short run (Miao et al. 2018; Hou & Chen 2026). This makes it partly a **treatment-intensity proxy**, not pure stress.
5. **Debt service cover:** weakest support for NSW councils. Debt falls or stays flat after disasters (Jerch et al.; Hou & Chen: debt levels decline). DRFA substitutes for borrowing (Melecky & Raddatz insurance logic), and many NSW councils carry little debt. I would down-weight it or keep it as a secondary or robustness indicator.

### 2. Expected sign and timing

| Indicator | Event FY (t) | t+1 | t+2 … t+5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating expenses and clean-up | ↑ (Audit Office 2023) | ↑ or partial reversal as operating grants arrive | Persistent ↑ in some functions (Liao & Kousky) |
| Operating performance ratio | ↓ likely | Ambiguous (catch-up grants) | Small ↓ via depreciation and impairment |
| Cash expense cover | ↓ (upfront funding) | ↓ or lowest point (claims pending more than 1 yr) | Recovers as DRFA is paid |
| Own-source revenue share | ↓ (grants in the denominator) | ↓ strongest as reconstruction grants flow | Returns towards baseline; possibly lower if the rate base shrinks (Jerch-type 6–10 yr effects) |
| Roads-and-bridges spending share | ↑ | ↑ (reconstruction peak; capital programs up to 2–3× normal) | Normalises |
| Debt service cover | ~0 | ~0 | ~0 / slight |

**FY alignment:** NSW fire seasons (Oct–Mar) fall *inside* one Jul–Jun FY, unlike California, where Liao & Kousky had to lag by one year. The event FY is the FY containing the ignition or burn dates. Fires in Jun or early Jul need special handling. Given DRFA claim lags, **pre-specify t, t+1 and t+2 windows** and report an event-study profile rather than a single-year difference. Jerch et al. show some damage emerges only at 6–10 years, beyond what a 2015–2025 panel can see for late fires.

### 3. Role of intergovernmental transfers (DRFA masking)
- Every sub-national study finds higher tiers absorb much of the cost: US states (Miao et al. 2018), US counties (Miao et al. 2023), US municipalities in the short run (Jerch et al.), and the hidden social-insurance cost (Deryugina 2017). In Australia, the Commonwealth reimburses up to 75% to states, which pass funds to councils for essential public asset restoration (PC 2014: "insurer of first resort"; ANAO: $3.1 billion 2018-19 to 2021-22).
- **Consequences for Y:** (i) Level-based council ratios **understate** the true fiscal cost of a fire. The cost moves to NSW and the Commonwealth, and to income support (Deryugina's channel). (ii) Transfers enter OLG ratios in ways that can *flip signs*: own-source share falls because of help, not harm. (iii) The locally borne residual is concentrated in **timing (cash), unreimbursable operating costs, deferral of other works, depreciation, and staff capacity**. That residual is largest for small, low-capacity rural councils (Royal Commission Ch. 11; Miao et al. 2023 heterogeneity).
- **Recommendations:** (a) Build FP from **changes vs own pre-event baseline, minus change in unburnt peer councils (the AUSSEF "excess")**, standardised within OLG council categories (Drew & Dollery). (b) Weight toward cash cover, operating balance and function-share reallocation; down-weight debt service. (c) Where available, add a separate transfer-intensity variable (DRFA or grant revenue per capita, or Δ grants and contributions). Use it as a control, or report "gross FP" (including transfers) alongside "net local FP", so masking is explicit rather than silently lowering the index. (d) Interact with pre-event reserves and council size to capture capacity-driven heterogeneity.
